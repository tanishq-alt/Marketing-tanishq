# Existing Desire and Demand

## Principle

Marketing is strongest when it channels a desire, fear, aspiration, frustration, or social motive that already exists. The job is rarely to invent a fundamental human desire from scratch.

## Desire model

Evaluate a candidate desire on four dimensions:

- **Intensity** — How painful or attractive is it now?
- **Frequency** — How often does it recur?
- **Scope** — How many viable buyers share it?
- **Spendability** — Are people willing and able to exchange money, time, data, reputation, or effort to satisfy it?

A desire can be emotionally strong but commercially weak if it has low spendability.

## Desire layers

Do not stop at functional need.

Example:

`feature → functional outcome → emotional outcome → identity/status outcome`

A project-management tool may offer reminders → fewer dropped tasks → less anxiety → feeling competent and dependable.

## Practical research

Look for:

- repeated complaints;
- workarounds;
- existing spend;
- recurring search behavior;
- communities organized around the problem;
- emotionally loaded customer language;
- costly avoidance behavior;
- status or identity implications.

## Strategy implications

If desire is strong but product awareness is low, lead with the desire/problem.
If desire is weak, stronger copy may not fix the market.
If multiple desires exist, choose one dominant desire for the primary message and use secondary desires as support.

## Failure modes

- leading with a product feature because the company is proud of it;
- assuming awareness equals desire;
- treating all benefits as equal;
- inventing urgency around a low-priority problem;
- confusing stated preference with demonstrated willingness to act.
