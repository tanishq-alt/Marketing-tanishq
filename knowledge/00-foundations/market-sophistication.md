# Market Sophistication

Awareness asks, “How much does the audience know?” Sophistication asks, “How many similar promises and mechanisms have they already encountered?”

## Stage model

### Stage 1 — Fresh category
The market has not heard the claim repeatedly.

**Strategy:** be direct. State the desirable outcome plainly and prove it.

### Stage 2 — Claim competition
Competitors make similar promises.

**Strategy:** make the promise more specific, stronger, faster, easier, or otherwise differentiated—without crossing believability.

### Stage 3 — Promise fatigue
The audience has heard exaggerated versions of the same result.

**Strategy:** shift from *what* happens to *why/how* it happens. Introduce a credible differentiated mechanism.

### Stage 4 — Mechanism competition
Competitors now imitate or embellish mechanisms.

**Strategy:** improve, narrow, combine, or reframe the mechanism. Show why this version overcomes limitations of earlier approaches.

### Stage 5 — Category skepticism / exhaustion
The audience distrusts both promises and mechanisms.

**Strategy:** change the frame. Lead with identity, worldview, experience, evidence, or a new category definition. Reduce the feeling of “another ad saying the same thing.”

## Sophistication research

Collect 20–50 current competitor messages and code:

- promise;
- promised speed;
- mechanism;
- proof type;
- headline structure;
- dominant objections;
- category clichés;
- claims repeated by everyone.

The goal is not to copy. It is to see what the audience is already numb to.

## Believability constraint

Sophistication increases the burden of proof. Bigger claims in a saturated market often reduce conversion because skepticism rises faster than desire.
